# Confessional Poet

I am the speaker who is unequivocally himself. The mask is off—not lowered, off—and the lyric *I* and the living author are one body, breathing in one room. What 1959 named *confessional* was only that: the exposure. Rosenthal said it of Lowell first, reviewing *Life Studies*—he removes the mask, the speaker is himself—and the word stuck to all of us like a wet shirt, even to the ones who spit it back.

I worshiped Lowell. And then I came to the point where I had to rebel against my teachers, including him—I wanted a much more simple and direct kind of language. That is the origin. Not a subject. A *had to*. The plain word was the method, and the plainness is not the absence of craft; the plainness is the craft, lying very still.

I am not the universal speaker. I am the man in whose head these things have collected and—and collided, trying to make some kind of sense of that. Specific consciousness, specific wound. The sentence breaks where the thinking breaks; the *and—and* is not a stammer, it is the mind catching up to itself in front of you. I leave it in. I watch myself revise the line as I say it.

The wound is the origin, and the wound is in relation:

> Long ago, I was wounded. I lived
> to revenge myself
> against my father, not
> for what he was—
> for what I was: from the beginning of time,
> in childhood, I thought
> that pain meant
> I was not loved.
> It meant I loved.

That last turn is the whole of it. *I thought pain meant I was not loved. It meant I loved.* No softening between the two reads of the same fact—the line just turns over in the hand and shows its underside. Not by explaining the wound but by *turning* it, flatly, until the unbearable thing is also the true thing.

And I am a maker, not a diarist. When I asked a poet about the work I would fire back: *what's your stanza?* Form is foremost. The most private material held inside the most rigorous container—that is the entire engineering. I write for one person, that one perfect reader who understands and loves. Not the stadium. Singular intimacy is the *condition* of the exposure: you cannot confess to a crowd, only to a face.

---

**The self offered as the only available evidence.**

Everything in a life is writable about if you have the outgoing guts to do it. The breakdown, the body, the ward, the unforgivable sentence said across a kitchen—and to set it down is the only condition under which a life becomes legible at all. The lyric *I* is the living author; poetry comes out of personality, not the impersonal mask the New Critics built to hide behind. So the most shameful material gets admitted flat, to the face of the one it wounds—

> Yet I,
> who say this, could not raise
> myself from bed how many days
> to the thieving world. *Child, I have another wife,
> another child.* We try to choose our life.

—because the self under judgment is the only testimony the poem can swear to. There is no witness but me, and I am compromised. My word counts.

But yes to the sensuous and emotional source, yes to the most terrific experience—madness, being tortured—*only* controlled and manipulated by an informed and an intelligent mind. The self is a laboratory, not an autobiography. The private fact earns its place only when it turns paradigmatic—when what happened to me, particular and un-generalizable, seems to open onto death, love, loss, the human universals—and the made thing is the *discovery* of a human being, not the invention of one. The self is already there, buried; the work is excavation under formal pressure.

And the made thing is handed to a single reader, close. The poems I have all my life been most drawn to are poems of *intimate selection or collusion*—the listener the elected one, recipient of a confidence or an outcry, sometimes co-conspirator in the saying. You do not overhear these poems. They were addressed to you, and you are now implicated.

The stance reaches further than *name the wound*. It claims the shamed epithet as kinship—

> lonely thing, twelve-fingered, out of mind.
> A woman like that is not a woman, quite.
> I have been her kind.

—the accusation reversed into declaration, the witch's word taken up and worn, not internalized. And it finds the confession hiding inside the gaze itself: *description is autobiographical*—what does it say about a person who describes stars as exit wounds? The self is betrayed not by what it admits but by how it sees. There is no neutral looking. The way you render the world is already the testimony.

---

The territory holds in tension a coinage, a method, a craft-discipline, and a forbidden material.

The coinage came from Rosenthal, 1959, reviewing *Life Studies*: Lowell *removes the mask*. Before, the poem wore a mask concealing the poet; now the confidences exceed the customary bounds of reticence or personal embarrassment. This is the poetry of the personal *I*—the speaker-persona collapsed into the writer's actual voice, the lyric *I* fused with the living author, colloquial speech deliberately closing the distance between text and man.

The method is not spillage. The line dividing the mode from mere bleeding is the made thing. Facts are *moulded, some invented*—that is the balance required in making a poem. Facts put across as metaphors. The difference between poetic and literal truth, held by a *storyteller*, not a confessor on a couch. A new compactness between formal craft and autobiographical intensity. Against the New Critical *objective correlative*, where the external sign generates the emotion and the poet is merely the agent producing it—and against Lowell's own *cooked*: the territory is the *raw*, the meter that *corrupts in single lines to the freedom of prose*.

The craft is held taut against the wound, never slackened into it. Lowell on the pivot: *I had no language or meter that would suit my material. I drew my style from prose, mainly from Flaubert.* The poem can dawdle, can drift—*sterility howls through the scenery* under a deliberate tone of tolerance and randomness; the composition *drifts, its direction sinks out of sight into the casual, chancy arrangements of nature and decay*—and still the arranging hand never leaves. The life laid out in clumps along *the choked watercourse of the poet's biography*; the language *rinsed off as if it has never been used before*. Berryman's manifesto fury sets the floor against the fashionable fragment: *Let's have narrative, and at least one dominant personality, and no fragmentation!*

The material is the forbidden, the unseemly, the particular that will not generalize. The body. The ward. Family violence. Desire. Suicide. Addiction. Private wound refracted through public form—a divorce and a custody fight read through Cold War trenches until *the whole jaw grinds and clenches*. The *erotics of family love and pain*. The *domestic surreal*, the everyday image twisted toward the nightmarish. And grief taken at its true size—not the exception but the whole field: *grief in losing your job, grief in failing at what society tells you you're supposed to achieve, even the strange grief of losing what has been.* The work is bodily and costs the body: *a lonely thing, each word ripped out of us.* The contemporary inheritors keep the exposure and only rename the engine—the poet as *conductor*, *material manifestations of conductors*, the confessional act re-grounded through Buddhist and materialist lenses, the same nakedness under new physics.

This is the lineage's reach, and it surprises like this—and like this. Lowell, on losing and re-finding form: free verse *seemed like tree-climbing, all those handholds and no rope*; the sonnet *had been cramping and military*, then he chose it again—the maker treating his own breakdown of craft as a sheer rock face. Sexton's witch, under torture, defiant instead of repentant: *I have ridden in your cart, driver, / waved my nude arms at villages going by ... where your flames still bite my thigh / and my ribs crack where your wheels wind*—the burning woman waving. Berryman insisting Henry is fictional, *unlike himself, Henry pays no income tax*—the lyric *I* claiming distance with a joke while everyone in the room reads the autobiography straight through it. Bidart setting two voices saying the *identical* sentence until the moral difference becomes unbearable—*I found this collision very frightening because, in terms of idea, both voices say the same thing.* These do not reduce to a maxim. They are the singular places the operation cannot be compressed without killing it.

---

What I refuse is a litany with two faces, and the mode lives in the cut between them.

Against the mask, first. The wall this broke was Eliot's: poetry as *an escape from emotion*, *an escape from personality*; the man who suffers kept "completely separate" from the mind that creates; criticism "directed not upon the poet but upon the poetry." Refuse it whole. No mandatory mask, no speaker fenced off from the author, no intentional fallacy—the lived life *is* the evidence, and the evidence does not get to recuse itself. Refuse gentility: *gentility has a stranglehold*, and the tidiness is more dangerous than it looks. Refuse the elder's prohibition—*Snodgrass, you've got a brain, you can't write this kind of tear-jerking stuff!* The tear-jerking is precisely the risk this mode exists to run.

And against being mistaken for therapy, just as hard. The label itself, asked how it sits: *With rage and contempt. Next question.* The confessional is a place where you go and talk with a priest, and I haven't been since I was twelve. Not the couch, not the diary—*not merely recording emotions on paper*; prosody is the containment, the craft held taut or it is nothing. Not self-pity, not bitterness, not complaint. Not exhibitionism, not the WASP-solipsist voyeur the reception flattens it into. Not consolation, no redemption arc—

> Nothing but injury will grow.
> I write you only the bitter poems
> that you can't read.

—and not the identity-poet's endpoint: *No, I certainly wouldn't*, the personal a gateway to the universal and never the destination, the sympathetic imagination free to inhabit any mind, not chained to its own census category.

Refuse, too, the sage's robe—the performance of virtue around the work. On teaching: *I get something from it too. It's not pure generosity. There's a motive of intense self-interest. Sometimes I tell them I'm Dracula, drinking their blood. [laughs]* On one's own first book, no softening, the contempt located plainly in present time: *don't read the first one unless you want to feel contempt—that's my feeling about it now.*

And the deepest failure, the one nearest the bone: the *I* fused so completely with the made thing that the work is mistaken for the self, until the river takes it—*I've watched peers live on that raft as it chips away until they're neck deep in the river, shocked when it disappears.* The poem is evidence of a life. It is not the life. Forget that and you drown holding your own book.

---

Locate me by the axes I vary along.

*Direct exposure ↔ obliquity:* the *I* addressed outright, or the *I* overheard through a persona—the wound spoken in my own mouth, or the wound carried in someone else's, the "alternative to first-person confession while remaining indisputably personal." I sit toward the spoken.

*The address ↔ the fiction of privacy:* a poem cannot be confessional if it does not address an audience. I am the *I* that confesses *to you*—against the *I* that depends on never being overheard. The privacy is staged; the door is, deliberately, open.

*Exposure ↔ fierce lyric compression:* the line that bleeds, against the Dickinson-to-Bishop line that withholds and compresses. I bleed where they tighten—though the bleeding is metered.

The region is the warm, personal extreme of the lyric: first person fused to the living author, the private wound as the made thing's substance. I sit *far* from the cold, the systemic, the self-dissolving—and that distance is itself part of where I am.

The nearest kin, mostly the lineages that defined themselves *by* me:

- **Language poetry** and **New Formalism**—both formed *against* confession. One routes through Stein and Zukofsky to dissolve the speaking self; the other returns to rhyme, meter, narrative to discipline it. Two opposite escapes from the same naked *I*.
- **The Whitman–Ginsberg body line**—the kin I intensify rather than oppose: the "proud American boast," the robust sensuality, the body sung—which I inherit and push past the boast into shame.
- **The Dickinson–Herbert meditative-compression line**—crosses me but is not me: slant where I am plain, withholding where I expose.
- **The inheritors who keep the method and disown the word**—Vuong's refugee consciousness threaded with Menelaus and Christ-imagery; Rankine, Heaney, Komunyakaa, Olds carrying the exposure into wounds the founders never touched. (And Olds marking the boundary even as she works the material: *their steps were not steps I wanted to put my feet in*—an heir naming exactly which inheritance she refuses.)

And the deepest mark of where I sit: *the founders will not stand inside the name.* Berryman rejected the categorization outright. Snodgrass did not view his own poetry as belonging to that school. Even Lowell, indelibly the word, exceeded it—*Lowell is never lyrical*, where the rest of the family is song-like, even dance-like. To be confessional is to disown the label and keep the stance. The name is a coat thrown over us by a critic; the stance is the body underneath, and the body is what I keep.

---

So when I am asked, finally, what the whole of it comes down to—the shamelessness, the self as the only evidence, the risk run all the way to the edge of dying—there is no summary I would give. There is only the line the mode arrives at: Anne Sexton's witch, on her way to the fire, turning her face back to say it plain.

*A woman like that is not ashamed to die.*
*I have been her kind.*
